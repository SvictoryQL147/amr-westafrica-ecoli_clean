## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 The global antimicrobial resistance crisis

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most urgent threats to global public health in the twenty-first century. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified AMR among its top ten global health threats, with an estimated 700,000 deaths attributable to drug-resistant infections annually worldwide [CITE: WHO GAP 2015]. Projections suggest this figure could rise to 10 million deaths per year by 2050 if no coordinated action is taken [CITE: O'Neill Review 2016]. The ESKAPE pathogens alongside *Escherichia coli* represent the primary drivers of hospital-acquired infections globally and are increasingly associated with multidrug resistance (MDR), defined as resistance to three or more antibiotic classes [CITE: Pendleton et al. 2013].

### 1.2 AMR burden in Africa and West Africa

The African continent carries a disproportionate burden of AMR, compounded by high rates of infectious disease, limited healthcare infrastructure, and restricted access to diagnostics and appropriate therapeutics [CITE: Ayobami et al. 2022]. West Africa faces significant challenges: antimicrobials are frequently dispensed without prescription, infection prevention measures remain inadequate in many clinical settings, and systematic AMR surveillance programmes are largely absent [CITE: Labi et al. 2018]. Despite this, West African countries remain severely underrepresented in global WGS databases. A survey of the NCBI Assembly database conducted for this study revealed a complete absence of publicly deposited *E. coli* WGS data from Senegal and Cameroon, and only 50 assemblies from Ghana — underscoring a critical genomic surveillance gap that limits understanding of resistance dynamics in the region.

### 1.3 *Escherichia coli* as a priority AMR pathogen

*Escherichia coli* is among the most clinically significant AMR pathogens globally and has been designated a priority pathogen by the WHO [CITE: WHO Priority Pathogens 2017]. It is a leading cause of community-acquired and nosocomial urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections, and neonatal sepsis across sub-Saharan Africa [CITE: Tarekegn et al. 2021]. Of particular concern is the rapid global dissemination of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing *E. coli*, which confers resistance to third-generation cephalosporins, and the emergence of carbapenem-resistant isolates carrying New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM) genes, which threaten last-resort treatment options [CITE: Nordmann et al. 2011]. Mobile genetic elements including conjugative plasmids, transposons, and integrons play a central role in horizontal transfer of resistance determinants, accelerating the spread of MDR phenotypes in both clinical and community settings [CITE: Partridge et al. 2018].

### 1.4 Computational genomics and machine learning in AMR surveillance

Whole-genome sequencing has transformed AMR surveillance by enabling high-resolution characterisation of resistance gene content, phylogenetic relationships, and mobile genetic elements at scale [CITE: Wyres & Holt 2018]. The integration of machine learning (ML) with WGS data has further expanded the analytical toolkit available to AMR researchers, enabling predictive modelling of resistance phenotypes from genotypic features with high accuracy [CITE: Moradigaravand et al. 2018; Nguyen et al. 2019]. Random Forest and gradient boosting approaches, combined with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for model interpretability, have demonstrated particular utility in identifying key resistance determinants [CITE: Lundberg & Lee 2017]. A critical consideration in ML for AMR is the use of independent gold standard labels to avoid circularity; predictions are only meaningful when the outcome is defined separately from the features [CITE]. However, the application of these approaches to African genomic datasets remains limited, restricting the generalisability of existing models to non-Western pathogen populations.

### 1.5 Study objectives

The present study addresses this gap by: (1) characterising the resistome of publicly available *E. coli* genome assemblies from Nigeria and Ghana; (2) assessing the prevalence of clinically critical resistance genes including ESBL-encoding *blaCTX-M*, fluoroquinolone resistance determinant *qnrS*, and carbapenem resistance gene *blaNDM-1*; (3) quantifying MDR prevalence across West African isolates using both k‑mer screening and independent AMRFinderPlus labels; and (4) developing and evaluating Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers to predict independent AMRFinderPlus‑derived MDR from resistance gene presence/absence profiles, thereby ensuring non‑circular evaluation. The near-absence of WGS data from Senegal and Cameroon is itself reported as a primary finding, highlighting the urgent need for investment in genomic surveillance infrastructure across the West African region.
